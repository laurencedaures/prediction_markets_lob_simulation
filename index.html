Where your P&L comes from

ComponentTotalPer contract

The three components are an exact identity, not an estimate: every trade is split into the spread you captured against the midquote at the time, how that midquote moved over the next 20 ticks, and whatever is left, which is your inventory carried to today. Adverse selection is negative when the market moves against you right after you are filled — the signature of trading with someone better informed.

Why the wings need a finer tick

A one-cent tick is a rounding error on a 50¢ contract and a blunt instrument on a 3¢ one. The chart shows what one tick is worth as a share of the contract's own price. The grid only binds where the curve is high: that is why Kalshi tapers the tick below 10¢ and above 90¢ rather than everywhere.

Guided exercises

They tick themselves as you trade.

    Application questions

    Each one is done in the simulator above. Try it before opening the answer.

    1. Read the grid
    Pause a market trading under 10¢ with tapered_deci_cent. What is the gap between two rows of the ladder? Switch to linear_cent and start a new market in the same range. What changes in the ladder, the spread and the “spread / price” figure?
    Inside the wings the rows step by 0.1¢; in the middle they step by 1¢, and the ladder marks the boundary at 10¢ and 90¢. Under linear_cent the rows are always 1¢ apart, the spread cannot be narrower than 1¢, and “spread / price” jumps: often 20–50% on a cheap contract against a few percent before.
    2. Quote inside the spread
    In a longshot market, use the + and − buttons to post a limit buy one tick above the best bid. Do it under both grids. How much do you improve the price, and how much do you have to pay when you later cross the spread?
    Under tapered_deci_cent you improve by 0.1¢ and still gain queue priority; under linear_cent the smallest improvement is a full cent, which on a 3¢ contract means paying 33% more. The coarse grid turns a marginal price improvement into an expensive one, so most traders simply queue behind.
    3. Measure the tick tax
    Run the comparison with Mostly longshots and favourites on, then off. Compare “spread / price” and the taker cost across grids in both cases.
    With the wings on, the finer grid cuts the relative spread sharply (roughly 20% down to 6% in a typical run) and lowers the taker's cost per contract. With the wings off, the three grids are nearly identical: at mid-range prices the desired spread already exceeds one cent, so the grid never binds. The taper earns its keep only where it applies.
    4. Split the cost in two
    Trade 100 contracts with a market order near 50¢, then near 3¢, with fees on. Compare the two components of your cost in the table: the distance over the midquote, and the fee.
    Near 50¢ the fee dominates and the half-spread is small. Near 3¢ it reverses: the fee is a fraction of a cent per contract while crossing the spread costs far more in relative terms. A trader who only looks at the published fee schedule will badly underestimate the cost of trading longshots.
    5. Grid and settlement
    Hold a longshot to settlement several times under each grid. Does the grid change whether you win? Does it change your P&L when you do?
    The grid does not change the outcome, which depends only on the true probability. It changes your entry price. Buying at 2.3¢ rather than 3¢ raises the payoff on a win from 32 to 42 times your stake, and cuts the loss when the contract expires worthless. Small absolute differences compound into large relative ones at the wings, which is exactly the argument for the taper.

    Glossary

    TickThe smallest price step the exchange allows. One cent on most Kalshi markets.

    Price gridThe set of prices an order may use. Kalshi publishes one per market.

    TaperUsing a finer tick near $0 and $1 than in the middle of the range.

    WingsPrices below 10¢ and above 90¢, where most Kalshi contracts trade.

    Effective half-spreadDistance between your trade price and the midquote before the trade.

    Time priorityAt one price, the order that arrived first trades first.

    The real Kalshi grids, and how this page simplifies them
    Kalshi publishes a price-level structure per market. linear_cent uses 1¢ across the range. tapered_deci_cent uses 0.1¢ below $0.10 and above $0.90, and 1¢ in between. Intermediate structures are named center_X_edge_Y_cent, where whole = $0.01, half = $0.005, quint = $0.002, deci = $0.001 and centi = $0.0001; most taper at $0.10 and $0.90. deci_cent uses 0.1¢ everywhere. Whole-cent prices are valid under every structure. This page implements four of them exactly, with the published taker fee. Everything else is a teaching simplification: one contract, a few hundred ticks, three kinds of bot (market makers who want a spread proportional to p(1 − p), retail takers who lean towards YES, and informed traders), and markets drawn so that about two thirds sit in the wings, as on Kalshi. No real market data is used.
